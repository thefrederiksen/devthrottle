Type · parent · ownership · state · colour · who is asked when a turn ends — the ONLY document that defines any of them

Everything about a session is defined here and nowhere else: what seat it holds, who made it, who it answers to, what it is doing, what colour that is, and who gets told when its turn ends. If a rule about a session is not in this document, it is not a rule.

1. The four facts about a session

Four different things get called "what this session is", and for two years the wrong ones were deciding who gets interrupted. They are separated here, named once, and each one says plainly whether it decides anything.

The shape of it, in one line. A session's type says what job it holds. Its parent says who made it. Its owner says who it answers to. Its colour says what it is doing right now.

Only the owner decides who is interrupted. Type and parent describe; they do not decide. Colour is computed from the rest and is never chosen by anybody.

Read the badges along the bottom and you have the history of this system's worst bugs. Every one was a fact that describes being allowed to decide — a seat stamped at birth outliving the supervisor it stood for, an origin silencing a scheduled run, a colour ladder that let "somebody else owns this" erase "this is working".

Why this separation is the whole design

Read the "does it decide" column downwards and you have the entire history of this system's worst bugs. Every one of them was a fact that described something being allowed to decide something.

  • The type decided, so a session stamped "Worker" at spawn stayed quiet for hours after the supervisor it worked for had exited. The seat outlived the relationship it stood for, and the documented orphan escape hatch could never fire.
  • The origin decided, so a scheduled run was silenced on the grounds that it escalates by email - a path nothing on the roster could attest had ever fired.
  • The colour decided, in the oldest form of this mistake: the July ladder put "somebody else owns this" two rows above "working", so a controlled session twenty-three minutes into real work reported slate and the work was discarded. See finding 1.

The rule that falls out of all three, and the one to hold onto: a category assigned at birth must never decide whether a session may reach the owner. The question is always about now - is somebody alive holding this - and it is asked fresh every pass. On 13 September 2026 six sessions sat grey and labelled "Snoozed" with nothing snoozed; five of the six had nobody who would ever come for them. That is what deciding by category looks like from the outside.

2. Ownership - who a session answers to

Every session has exactly one owner, and it is either another session or the user. There is no third answer and there is no unowned session: no owner means the user.

Parent is inferred. Ownership is chosen. They are not the same edge.

These two are confused constantly because on an ordinary spawn they carry the same session id. They are different questions and they come apart on purpose.

Why the refusal. cc-devthrottle session spawn used to read:

elif cc_session: controller_session_id = cc_session

So a session that spawned without saying anything took ownership of what it spawned, because CC_SESSION_ID happened to be set in its environment. Nobody chose it and nothing recorded that a choice had been made. That default was the only way work could stop being the user's - a person spawning and a schedule firing both land on him already, so a session spawning a session is the single path by which something leaves his queue. It is now the single path that has to declare itself.

A human spawn declares nothing, and that is not an exemption. There is nothing to state: a session a person opens from the desktop, the Cockpit or the phone is the user's, and there is no second candidate to tell it apart from. The requirement lands exactly where the ambiguity is.

--standalone stays, and it is not a loophole

The owner settled this on 14 September 2026 when it was put to him that a session started by another session should never be allowed to hand itself to the user. He kept the choice, and the reasoning is the point:

"Stand-alone should not disappear, because there are cases where the user asked to start a new session that is standalone, or an agent can start a session that should pop up to the user. But the agent should clearly understand this, and they should be forced to choose."

Two legitimate cases, and in neither is the spawning session shirking:

  • The user asked for it. He told a session to open another for him. That session made the call, but the work was never its own.
  • The session is starting something for him. Work that genuinely should reach him rather than be collected by the session that started it.

So the rule is not "a session may never give work to the user". It is that a session can never arrive there by accident. Forced to choose, knowingly - not forced into one answer. The failure being closed was a silent default, and a default is exactly what an explicit declaration is not.

Handing work to the user is a deliberate act, so it states a reason

--standalone and --controlled-by self cost the same to type. Measured on 14 September: three of thirteen agent-started sessions had chosen --standalone, and two of those were sitting red at the owner. So from inside a session it now requires --why.

It does not forbid the choice - there are real cases, and section 2 names them. It makes an agent that cannot say why the work is the owner's collect it itself. The reason is not yet carried on the wire, and that is a real limit rather than an oversight: there is no field for it on the create, so it is printed with the result and lands in the spawning session's own transcript. A field on the session is the right home and is not built.

3. Who is asked when a turn ends

A session goes quiet only when something alive is actually holding it.

The rule

When a turn finishes, a session asks exactly one question: is there a live owner right now?

  • No. Then it goes RED and asks the user. It does not matter what seat it holds or how it was started.
  • Yes. Then it stays quiet, and it tells that owner itself - because the owner asked it to do something, and getting back to them is part of doing it.

That is the whole rule. In code it is one line, and the line is the point:

IsSupervised(s) => s.HasLiveSupervisor

No seat. No origin. One fact, read fresh every pass.

It fails toward the owner. HasLiveSupervisor defaults to false, so a path that folds without resolving the fleet first answers "not supervised" and the session SURFACES.

Read the two error directions, because the asymmetry is deliberate. The wrong answer this can give is a session asking for him when somebody had it - a nuisance. The wrong answer it cannot give is silence over a session nobody had - which is the failure that cost six sessions on 13 September.

The report is the last step of the work, not a notification

This is the half that did not exist in any form before 13 September 2026, and it is the centre of the change. A session with an owner was asked to do something. Finishing includes getting back to whoever asked. So the report is an action the session takes, in its own words, saying what it did - not the Gateway inferring from a grey dot that somebody ought to go and look.

  • Quiet becomes earned. A session is allowed to be grey only because a message was actually sent to somebody alive. No message, no grey.
  • An owner has a queue too. Being an owner means receiving these reports, which is what makes delegation real rather than decorative.
  • It is QUEUED for the owner, never typed into it (owner's ruling, 16 September 2026, which REPLACES the ruling of 13 September 2026). On 13 September the owner ruled that a report interrupts deliberately: every fleet message landed mid-turn in the receiving session, on the reasoning that a session which took ownership took on being interrupted. Three days later he reversed it: "Messages interrupt what we do and it's really detrimental to the other sessions." No agent interrupts another any more, with or without a grant. A report is a record in the owner session's inbox on the Gateway; when that session is not working and its composer is empty, its Director types one fixed doorbell line telling it to run cc-devthrottle message inbox, and reading marks the report read. What the 13 September ruling was protecting still holds, because the record is not pull-only the way NeedsManager is: an unread report is rung again after five minutes, marked stuck after three rings with a notice to the sender, and the owner session's row shows "N messages waiting" on every screen. A signal nobody is obliged to read is a signal that does not exist - so the doorbell keeps ringing until it is read. See docs/FleetMessaging.md and the Message Load mission record (docs/missions/message-load-2026-09-16/).
  • With no live owner it sends nothing, and succeeds. No owner means the user, and a session the user owns is already red and already in his queue the moment it stops - that red IS the report.
  • It refuses rather than guess. "Do I have an owner?" is answered from the fleet, so a roster that could not be read in full is not evidence of having none - it is not knowing. An absence-shaped check failing open here would turn every roster hiccup into "you are the user's", and a worker would silently stop reporting to a supervisor that was alive throughout.

How the report is actually sent

The rule above is only real because there is a verb for it:

cc-devthrottle session report "<what you did, in your own words>"
cc-devthrottle session raise  "<what you are blocked on>"   # still working, need a decision
cc-devthrottle session raise  --clear                          # the decision was answered
cc-devthrottle session workers                                  # what I own, and who has a hand up

session report is the last step of delegated work, not a courtesy, and it resolves the owner itself - so a session never has to know whether it has one. With no live owner it sends nothing and succeeds, because the red already in the user's queue IS the report.

session raise is the other direction and a different moment: not "I have finished" but "I cannot go on without an answer", raised while still working. The hand lowers itself when the session stops. The owner never sees a raised hand - it is not read by the colour, the label or the triage bucket, and a test asserts it changes none of the three. The supervisor-facing surface is session workers, the command line, which is where supervisors on this fleet actually live.

Three outcomes, and every one has a named recipient. Nothing in the fleet finishes a turn and tells no one. The question is about NOW, which is why it is asked fresh every turn and never stamped at birth.

What it replaced, and the measurement that prompted it

Before 13 September 2026 the inputs were the seat, the origin, and - in one case out of ten - the owner's liveness, skipped entirely whenever a seat had been assigned by hand. Seven of ten cases were silenced. One of those seven had a live owner. The other six were quiet because of a category, and even in the one legitimate row the owner was never told: it would have had to go looking.

The shift is from type to relationship. The fleet used to quieten a session because of what kind of thing it was. It now quietens a session only because somebody real is holding it.

4. The seats

What a session is FOR. Four seats, and since 13 September 2026 none of them decides who is interrupted - which is what makes them safe to use for what they are good at: naming the job and grouping the fleet.

The four

SeatHow it is assignedWhat it means
Standalone The default. Derived: not a worker, not an architect, controls nothing live. An ordinary session. Nothing about ordinary use changes.
Manager Derived from the spawn graph: a non-worker, non-architect that supervises at least one live session. A coordinator. It becomes a Manager the instant it has a live worker and reverts to Standalone when the last one exits. Learns worker status by READING them, not by being messaged "notice me".
Worker Derived: it names a supervisor. A session doing a piece of work for another. A Worker that spawns sub-workers keeps the Worker label and is not relabelled - nesting works, and a mid-tree session simply shows the Worker badge.
Architect EXPLICIT only. Never derived - architecture cannot be inferred from the spawn graph. A design seat. It recommends and maintains the design documents; it does not implement. It is human-facing (owner's ruling, 6 September 2026): "the architect is always the session i talk to."

Resolution precedence: an explicitly-set seat wins; otherwise the seat derives from the spawn graph, in the order Worker (has a supervisor) → Manager (supervises a live session) → Standalone.

An explicit seat is sticky, and that used to be dangerous. Because the resolver returned an explicit stamp before the liveness check, a seat stamped "Worker" at spawn outlived the supervisor it stood for, and the orphan escape hatch could never fire. That is fixed by the seat no longer deciding anything - but the stickiness itself is unchanged, so do not reintroduce a rule that reads the stamped seat to answer a question about now.

One axis only - there is no job-type

DECIDED (owner, 9 July 2026): the fleet uses ONLY the management axis. There is no separate job-type axis, and the command line once carried a --type option (Developer / Implementation / Discuss / Product / QA / Support) that the server never read; it was removed on 9 July 2026, in the same change that removed the guesswork it encouraged.

Raising a hand - NeedsManager

A supervised session can raise its hand to its supervisor while still working (issue #2662, BUILT). Set it when all of these hold: the session is still actively Working; continuing without a decision would be wrong or wasteful (an ambiguous requirement, a destructive step, a real design fork, an authorization it does not hold); and it cannot resolve the decision within its own mandate.

  • The hand lowers itself when the session stops working - derived, not swept - because stopping already cues the supervisor, and a latch somebody has to clear is the one still up next week.
  • The reason is required. A hand with no words is the "notice me" ping this design rejects.
  • The owner never sees it. It is not read by the colour, the label or the triage bucket, and a test asserts a raised hand changes none of the three.
  • Do NOT set it for routine progress or for "I finished" - going quiet covers that.

Two layers of attention - do not conflate them

EffectiveColor is ONE value per session, shown to ALL viewers. So "a worker glows toward ITS supervisor" - which only that supervisor should see - cannot live in EffectiveColor. Attention splits into two layers:

  1. Global, human-facing - the colour, label and triage bucket, owned by the single Gateway fold. One value everyone sees. This is where "supervised sessions do not nag the human" is enforced.
  2. Viewer-relative, supervisor-facing - a "this session wants me" highlight, computed locally by each client from the facts the fold exposes, shown only to the viewer who IS that session's supervisor.

STILL NOT BUILT, and this document should not be read as claiming otherwise: no client draws the viewer-relative highlight. No client knows its own session id, so none can ask "am I this row's supervisor?". The supervisor-facing surface that WAS built for issue #2662 is the command line - cc-devthrottle session workers - which is where supervisors actually live on this fleet. A rail highlight remains open.

A supervised session is not the owner's to be read aloud

The wingman does not enrol a supervised session into voice mode, and it also switches one back off. Both passes exist. The rule had to RESOLVE the seats itself rather than read the field, because the push store nulls the resolved seat at ingest - a check that merely read it would answer "not supervised" for every session on the fleet and narrate exactly as before, which would have read as "the change had no effect" on precisely the fleet that prompted it.

The off-direction is deliberately NOT gated on the fleet voice switch. That switch answers "does this tenant want voice?"; this answers "is this session the owner's to be read?" - and a supervised session is not his to be read whether the switch is on or off. Gating it would leave a tenant who had since turned voice mode off with a set of marked sessions that nothing ever clears.

The one accepted cost, named rather than discovered. The sweep's on-direction is deliberately one-way so it never fights somebody who put ONE session on voice by hand. The off-direction DOES fight that person, in exactly one case: they put voice on a supervised session deliberately and this turns it back off. Accepted - being able to hand-mark a session into a state the rule forbids is not a capability worth preserving. It touches nothing else: a Manager or a Standalone marked for voice is never in that list.

A handover is a PEER, and never inherits a supervisor

The handover and move-session path does not go through spawn and never sets a supervisor. Its target is a CONTINUATION of the source - a peer, not a subordinate - so if it were auto-supervised the moved session's red would be suppressed toward the user and he would lose sight of work he is still holding. The source session is deliberately not recorded as the parent either: lineage means "who asked for this", and nobody asked for a handover target; the work simply moved.

An orphan escalates with an EXPLANATION, and is never reassigned

A session whose supervisor died is nobody's, so it reaches the user - that is the escape hatch, and it falls out of the rule rather than being a special case. Two details survive from the 9 July 2026 ruling and still bind:

  • It surfaces with a clear explanation, not just a red dot: the supervisor it was working for is gone, here is the task, here is where it is stuck. The user then decides - resume, reassign, hand off, or stop. The owner's standing rule for exceptions is that an exception always involves him.
  • It is NEVER auto-reassigned to another supervisor (the owner's veto of the reassign-to-a-hub-manager proposal). That would mask the problem and need always-on machinery to do it. A session that finished cleanly needs no escalation at all; it is the BLOCKED orphan that has to reach somebody.

The design goal that minimises the whole case: a supervisor shutting down CLEANLY deals with what it owns first - finish, hand off, or stop - so orphaning only ever comes from a true crash.

Permissions are the user's choice, per session

DECIDED (owner, 9 July 2026): permission and safety posture is the user's call, for every seat including Worker. The framework does NOT force, gate, or nag about it. We are not a seatbelt chime.

6. The machine

Three layers. Each has exactly one job. The whole design is that no layer does another layer's job.

The sensor's state machine

These are the only session states that exist. The Director writes exactly three of them (Working, WaitingForInput, Exited); everything else on any screen is a presentation of these facts, not a state of its own.

Read the diagram and the law together. There are only two things a running session can be: it is producing bytes (Working, blue), or it has been silent for ten seconds (WaitingForInput, red). Everything else in this document - grey, orange, yellow, purple, slate - decorates the right-hand box. None of them can touch the left one. That is what "nothing outranks working" means, drawn.

The snooze machine, and the only two things that clear a snooze

The owner's rules, 14 July 2026.

  1. Working knocks a session out of snooze. Always. Amended 17 September 2026 - see below: work the Director attributes to another agent's send does not.
  2. An expired snooze is gone. Set it for twelve hours; twelve hours later there is no snooze.
  3. Snooze pressed while working means "snooze me when the work ends" - otherwise an agent could never snooze its own session, because an agent asking for a snooze is by definition working at that moment.
  4. Nothing else clears a snooze. Only: it starts working, you unsnooze it, or the timer expires.

The owner's decision, 17 September 2026 - the exception to rule 1. Work that the Director attributes to an agent-origin send - a fleet doorbell, or any other text the product typed on an agent's behalf - does NOT end an armed snooze. The owner's own work (typing or speaking into the session) still ends it, and so does work nothing explains (the agent starting on its own, a repaint, an older Director that does not say where the work came from).

Why: a snooze is the owner's wish to be left alone. Another agent ringing the session must not undo that wish and turn his screen red again. The Message Load mission made every fleet message a queued record announced by a doorbell line, and a doorbell makes the session work, so under the July wording every doorbell would have ended the snooze. The owner chose the recommendation: keep the exception in its narrow form.

How it works: the Director reports WorkingOrigin (owner, agent, or nothing) on the session it pushes, set at the one place every submission passes and cleared when the session settles. SnoozeLandingObserver keeps an armed snooze when the origin is exactly agent, and logs it; the owner-turn edge still runs first. A detector flicker that clears the origin mid-turn makes the next working push unexplained, so the error falls on the side of ending the snooze, which is the July law's side.

Background-quiet is a SEPARATE mechanism - not snooze surviving work. Rule 1 does not mean "an agent may never churn quietly in the background." The owner accepts that agents working away without needing him is a real need. But it must NOT be met by letting a snooze live through work: a snooze is the owner's "not now", and the moment there is activity on that terminal the snooze is spent. Background-quiet is a distinct "background / running" state, a later mission - it is purpose-built for "let it run silently", where snooze is purpose-built for "I have parked this." Do not weaken rule 1 to approximate the other; the two are different states with different meanings, and collapsing them is how the working edge came to be dropped in the first place.

Rule 2 used to fail on the deferred path - defect 20, and it was the worst thing in this document. FIXED 14 July 2026. Keep reading: the mechanism is recorded because it is the shape of mistake this whole system keeps making, not because the bug is still there.

What went wrong. A deferred hold reports OnHold=false (correctly - it is not parked yet). The expiry sweep ran every 15 seconds, asked the Director "is it held?", heard no, and deleted the 12-hour timer. Minutes later the turn ended, the deferral landed, and the session was held with no clock. An agent-requested snooze never expired - and since an agent snoozing its own session is by definition working when it asks, that was the case, not an edge case.

The two rules collided because the wire was lossy: three hold states squeezed into one boolean (defect 12), so "not held" and "about to be held" were indistinguishable to the sweep.

THE RULING, 14 July 2026: the clock starts when the work ENDS. "Snooze me for 12 hours when this finishes" means twelve hours of quiet after it finishes. A clock started at request time could expire before the hold has even landed.

What shipped, in one sentence each:

  1. All three hold states cross the wire on SessionDto.HoldState, and OnHold is derived from it (defect 12 - the root cause).
  2. The hold endpoint reads HoldResponse.Pending and, on a deferral, records the snooze's length with no deadline - it does not start a clock.
  3. The clock starts when the hold lands: SnoozeLandingObserver on the Director's push (prompt), SnoozeExpirySweep as the backstop (certain).
  4. The sweep clears a timer only on a true None - never on a DeferredHold, and never merely because something reads "not held".

The rule this defect leaves behind, for whoever touches the snooze next. If you are about to make the sweep read a boolean again, or clear a snooze because a session "is not held" - stop. That is not a simplification, it is the defect, and it is written down here because it survived review, shipped, and was invisible for as long as the only thing anybody asked was "is it held?".

Three states exist because three things are true: nothing is held; something is held; something has been ASKED FOR and has not landed yet. Any encoding that cannot say the third one will lose it - silently, and only in production, and only for the feature's main use.

NEVER WRITE A CAUSE YOU HAVE NOT OBSERVED. This rule is here because this document broke it.

An earlier version of defect 19 stated that the wedged orange was "almost certainly" caused by the Gateway running out of credits, and called it "the everyday way in". That was invented. Nobody had run out of credits. No log was read, no balance was checked, no pending record was examined. A plausible-sounding cause was then attached to a real symptom and written down as near-fact.

That is the most dangerous thing anyone can do to this document, and it is worse than the bug it described. A cited defect is checkable. A fabricated cause is not - it reads exactly like a finding, it survives review because it sounds reasonable, and the next agent implements against a world that does not exist. Someone has now checked, and this is how the story ends - it is worth reading twice. On 14 July 2026 the logs were correlated (2402 files, 824 upload ids). The standard for this document: a mechanism may be stated from reading the code, and must be cited by file and line. A cause may only be stated from evidence you have actually observed - a log line, a record on disk, a reproduction. If you have not looked, the honest sentence is "this is undiagnosed; here is how to find out." Write that sentence. It is worth more than a confident guess, because it tells the reader the truth about what is known.

Only four things have ever actually caught one. They are mechanical on purpose. Do these to every claim in this document, and to every claim anyone hands you - including one from the Architect, including one you wrote yourself an hour ago.

  1. Try to REFUTE the claim, not confirm it - and by a DIFFERENT route than the one that produced it. Re-running someone's grep re-runs their mistake. Killed error 5: "nothing assigns Idle" was proven by enumerating the only two ActivitySink producers and reading what they emit - not by grepping again.
  2. Prove the CONSUMER, never the call site. "Nothing calls it" and "nothing that PAINTS reads its output" are different claims and need different proofs. Before deleting anything, ask what ASSIGNS the value and what READS it - and "never assigned" does not mean "safe to delete", it may mean a live reader is already broken. Killed error 6, which would have deleted a producer with five live consumers.
  3. Prefer the argument that does not depend on a comment. A change justified by "equivalent today" dies if the equivalence is false. The same change justified by "the consuming fold already requires raw red, so this gate is redundant" survives either way. If your only reason is something a comment told you, you have no reason. Killed error 7 - an unproven "iff" offered as justification, declined.
  4. Re-count every count, re-read every line number, before you repeat it. A count is a fact with a shelf life; this branch rots them within hours. Errors 3, 7 and 8 are all inheritance - someone repeating a number nobody re-checked.
  5. A principle is not verified until its IMPLEMENTATION is - in ITS runtime. Being right about the rule tells you nothing about whether the code you wrote obeys it. Probe the runtime; do not reason from the principle.
