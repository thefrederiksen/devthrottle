1. The shape in one paragraph

A company buys DevThrottle and gets a team. The team has an owner and one or more development managers. They write the rules the team's agents run under - what may be started, what may be spent, what may be said, what may be kept, and what may leave the building. The Gateway and the Director enforce those rules at the moments they can, and record what they did at every moment, including the ones where all they could do was watch. The manager reads the result on a Governance tab in the Cockpit, and gets it as a report on a schedule. That last sentence is the product. The rules are the mechanism; the answerable record is what a company is actually buying.

The distinction worth keeping straight. A policy is one rule. Governance is the system around the rules: who may set them, who may override them, what is written down, who reads it, and who is answerable when it goes wrong. Our closest competitor shipped the rules. Nobody has shipped the system.

2. Who sets a policy - the roles ladder

Four levels. Each one can only make the rules stricter than the level above it. This is the single most important design decision in the whole feature, and it is the opposite of how the competitor built theirs: in their engine the end user is evaluated first and can short-circuit, which means the person being governed gets the first word. In a team product that is not governance, it is a suggestion.

LevelWhoWhat they setMay they loosen the level above?
CompanyOwnerThe rules nobody in the company can escape - what is never stored, what never leaves, the ceiling on spend, which agents and models are allowed at all.No. This is the floor.
TeamDevelopment managerRules for this team's work - repositories, machines, branches, the team's own spend cap, what needs a human first.No - only tighten
AgentWhoever authors an agent definitionRules that travel with that agent wherever it runs.No - only tighten
SessionThe person doing the workExtra care for this one piece of work - "ask me before anything destructive today".No - only tighten

Two things fall out of "tighten only". The first is that an override is a separate, recorded act, not a policy edit: if a developer needs past a manager's rule, they ask, a named person grants it, and that grant has a scope and an expiry and sits in the record forever. The second is that the roles are not a list of permission checkboxes - they are a chain of answerability. When the report says something was allowed, it can say whose rule allowed it.

The role list itself

RoleCan set policyCan overrideReads the reportNotes
OwnerCompany levelAnything, recordedYesOwns billing and the ceiling. There is always exactly one answerable person.
Development managerTeam levelWithin their team, recordedYes - this is who the report is written forAdds and removes people, assigns machines and repositories.
DeveloperTheir own agents and sessionsNo - may request, cannot grantTheir own activity onlyThe person the governance is about.
AuditorNoNoEverything, read onlyThe seat a security review or a customer's compliance officer sits in. Sells the enterprise tier on its own.

3. What a policy is, and the four verdicts

A policy has five parts, and a person should be able to read one out loud: who it applies to, where it is checked, what it is looking for, what happens when it finds it, and what gets written down.

The competitor has three verdicts. We need four, because you said it yourself: in some places we can stop it, and in some places we can only warn about it. Pretending a warning is a block is the lie that would make the whole feature untrustworthy.

VerdictWhat happensWho feels it
AllowIt proceeds. Still recorded when the policy asks to be - "this was checked and was fine" is evidence too.Nobody
WarnIt proceeds, and it is recorded as a breach, counted, surfaced on the tab and in the report. Used where we found out too late to stop it.The manager, later
AskIt stops and waits for a named person to say yes or no. Their answer is recorded against their name.The developer, now
BlockIt does not happen. The agent is told why, in plain English.The agent and the developer, now

Two rules bind all four, and neither is negotiable:

  • An engine that cannot decide must never allow. If a check errors, times out, or the database is unreachable, the verdict is Block or Ask - never a quiet Allow. A policy engine that fails open is worse than no policy engine, because it sells a promise it then drops silently.
  • Every policy is born in dry run. It records what it would have done and does nothing, and a named person moves it to live. We already do exactly this for standing instructions in the product, and it is the only way a manager can turn on a strict rule without stopping the team's work on a Monday morning.

4. Where we can actually intercept - the honest map

This is the part of your question I want to answer carefully, because the answer is not uniform. We have two enforcers and they see different things.

The Gateway is a server. It sees every request that crosses it and it owns the database, so it is the only place that can govern what is kept. The Director runs on the machine. It starts the agent, owns the pipe that types into it, reads the terminal screen, and installs the agent's own hook files. Between them, here is what we can and cannot do:

Interception pointEnforcerStrongest verdict availableWhat it covers
Starting a sessionDirector and GatewayBlockWhich agent, which model, which repository, which machine, who asked for it, and how many at once.
The prompt or message going inBoth - we own the pipeBlockEvery word that reaches an agent from a person, a schedule, or another session. Checked before a key is typed.
MoneyGatewayBlockSpend per session, per person, per team, per day. Enforced at the turn boundary.
Anything written to the recordGatewayBlock, before the writeTranscripts, logs, handovers, notes, briefs, reports. This is our strongest point and the one nobody else has.
Anything leaving the building through our own toolsBothBlockEmail, issues and pull requests, posts, push notifications, messages between sessions.
What the agent decides to do - its individual tool callsDirector, through the agent's own hooksBlock only where that agent offers a blocking hook; otherwise nothingShell commands, file writes, network calls. Today we install one observational hook and read no tool calls at all. This is real work, it differs per agent, and it is the seam that breaks when the agent vendors ship a new version.
The terminal screen, as it changesDirectorWarn only, after the factEverything - whatever the agent read, wrote, printed or was told. The widest net we have, and always the latest.
Turn endGatewayBlock the next turnCaps, stopping a session that has gone wrong, requiring a human before it continues.
The sentence to remember. The earlier we catch something the more we can stop; the later we catch it the more we can see. A serious policy uses both ends - block where we own the door, warn where we only have a window - and says which one it was, every time, to the manager's face.

5. Worked example: "no personal data in our sessions"

You asked how we would create this one. Here is the honest walk-through, which is also the template for every policy of this shape.

Where it could appearWhat we can doVerdict
In a prompt somebody types or dictatesWe own the pipe. We check the text before it is typed.Block or Ask
In a file the agent opened on its ownWe do not see it unless that agent reports its tool calls to us.Warn, once it reaches the screen
On the terminal screenWe read the screen. We find out after it happened.Warn, recorded and counted
In anything we store - transcript, log, handover, reportThe Gateway is the writer. Nothing is stored until the check has run.Block the write, or store it redacted with the redaction recorded
In anything going out - an email, an issue, a commit, a postThose go through our own tools.Block

So "no personal data in our sessions" is not one promise. It is three honest ones, and they are worth more than one dishonest one:

  1. It is never stored by us unredacted.
  2. It never leaves through anything we control.
  3. It is flagged and counted the moment it appears on a screen, and the manager sees it.

What we cannot promise is that an agent never lays eyes on it, and we should say so in those words. A vendor who claims that fourth promise is either sandboxing the whole machine or lying.

6. Governed storage - the half nobody has

Everyone's policy engine governs what an agent may do. Nobody governs what is kept. We own the ledger end to end, so this is ours to take, and it needs no competitor to exist first.

  • Redaction on the way in. The check runs before the write, not as a cleanup job afterwards. A cleanup job means the unredacted version existed, and in a compliance conversation that is the only fact that matters.
  • Every redaction is itself a record. What was found, which rule found it, what was put in its place, when, and under whose policy. A redaction nobody can see is indistinguishable from data loss.
  • Reversible where the company says so. Some teams need the original held under a stricter key; some need it gone. That is a policy, set once.
  • Retention. How long each kind of record lives - transcripts, screens, logs, reports - per company, with a legal hold that overrides deletion and is itself recorded.
  • Answering for one person. Show me everything you hold about this individual; remove it. This is a feature, not a support ticket, and it is table stakes the moment a real company buys.
One hard constraint that overrides any convenience here. The spoken-words rule in this repository binds every path that turns audio into text: the raw transcript is the speech model's output word for word, and the cleaned version may differ only by corrections the user listed by hand and by whole sentences a silence gate dropped, each one recorded. A personal-data policy over stored dictation must therefore be a recorded, answerable edit of exactly that shape - never a rewrite, never a quiet shrink. If the design cannot be made to fit that, the design changes, not the rule.

7. The record, and why it is the product

Every verdict is written down, including Allow. The row says what was being attempted, which policy looked at it, what it decided, why, at which level that policy was set and by whom, who overrode it if anyone did, and what was recorded in place of what was blocked.

We already have most of the shelf for this - an append-only governance log that already carries permission decisions, a session ledger, and a spend ledger. The policy engine should write into that furniture rather than build a second one beside it. An append-only record is the whole basis of the claim; a record you can edit is not evidence.

The test of whether we built this right is one sentence: can a manager ask a question in plain English and get an answer out of the record? "Did anything with personal data get stored last month?" "Who approved the deployment on the fourteenth?" "Which rule is stopping this team the most?" If those need an engineer, we have built a log and not a governance system.

8. The governance report to the manager

On a schedule the manager chooses. Written for a person who was not watching, so it leads with what needs them and never with a table of counts.

  • What needs you. Requests waiting for a decision, overrides about to expire, rules stuck in dry run that somebody asked for weeks ago.
  • What was stopped. Blocks and asks, grouped by rule, with the two or three that matter named in a sentence each.
  • What we could only warn about. Kept separate, never mixed into the stopped pile. This is the honest column, and it is also the useful one - it is where the next thing worth building shows itself.
  • Who overrode what, and whether it has expired.
  • Spend against the cap, per team and per person.
  • Drift. Rules that never fire and probably should go; rules that fire constantly and are really a broken process; people who are blocked every day and need a conversation rather than a tighter rule.

Same content on the tab and in the report - the report is the tab, addressed to somebody and sent. One source, not two things that drift apart.

9. The Governance tab on the Cockpit

A new top-level tab, not a page buried in settings. Settings is where you configure a thing; Governance is where you watch it. Sections, in the order a manager wants them:

SectionWhat is on it
Needs youAsks waiting on a human, override requests, expiring grants. When it is empty it says so plainly rather than showing an empty table.
PoliciesEvery rule in force, grouped by level, each showing who set it, when, whether it is live or in dry run, and how often it has fired. Adding one is a sentence plus a scope, not a configuration file.
ActivityThe verdict stream. Filter by person, team, repository, rule, verdict. This is what an auditor is shown.
People and rolesWho is in the team, what role they hold, what they may set and override.
StorageWhat we hold, for how long, what has been redacted, what is on legal hold. The governed-storage half made visible - otherwise it is invisible work nobody pays for.
ReportsThe schedule, the recipients, and every report we have sent, kept.

The phone gets the same tab with the same sections in the same order and reduced cards - the rule in this repository is that a screen exists once and both surfaces mount it, because two lists in two files drift apart by default. On the phone, "Needs you" is the whole point: approving an ask from a queue at a traffic light is the best demonstration of this feature we will ever have.

10. What version one should be

My recommendation, to argue with:

  1. The engine and the record first - four verdicts, the four levels with tighten-only, fails closed, every verdict recorded in the governance log, every policy born in dry run.
  2. Block where we own the door - starting a session, the prompt going in, spend, what is stored, what leaves. All of these are ours today and need no cooperation from any agent vendor.
  3. Warn where we only have a window - the terminal screen. One watcher, honestly labelled, which is what makes a personal-data policy meaningful rather than theatre.
  4. Governed storage as the headline - redaction before the write, recorded, with retention. The half nobody has, and the half that does not need us to win a feature race.
  5. The tab and the report, because a policy nobody reads the results of is not governance.
  6. Not in version one: reaching inside a running agent to gate its individual tool calls. It is a blocking hook per agent family, only some of them offer one, and it is exactly the brittle seam we point at as the competitor's weakness. It gets its own phase with its own honest cost.

11. Decisions waiting on you

A. Does the ladder tighten only, so a developer can never loosen a manager's rule?

Recommended: yes, tighten only, with an override as a separate recorded grant. The competitor evaluates the end user first, which means the governed party gets the first word. That is fine for a single-player tool and wrong for a team product. Overturn this and the feature becomes a convenience rather than something a company can point an auditor at.

B. Four verdicts including Warn, rather than the three everyone else has?

Recommended: four. You said it in your own words - some places we stop it, some places we warn. Without Warn, everything we can only see after the fact has to be dressed up as a block or dropped entirely, and both of those are lies to the manager.

C. Is Governance its own top-level tab, or a section inside Settings?

Recommended: its own tab. Settings is where you configure; Governance is where you watch. It is also the screen we demonstrate the whole team tier from, and a screen you sell from does not live three clicks down.

D. Does version one gate only what the Gateway and Director already own, leaving tool-call interception to a later, named phase?

Recommended: yes. What we own is real and unique - refuse the session, refuse the prompt, cap the spend, govern the record forever. Reaching inside a running agent buys the competitor's fragility in order to match a feature. The one thing this scope cannot demonstrate is a shell command stopped mid-run, so if that demonstration is what sells it, this is the one to overturn.

E. Who is the report actually written for - the development manager, or the owner?

Recommended: the development manager, with the owner getting a shorter roll-up across teams. One document written for two readers ends up serving neither, and the manager is the one who can act on what is in it.
