Mission - Session Cards

Status: ACTIVE. Branch mission/session-cards, cut from origin/main at v2.8.0. Opened 19 September 2026. How a mission is run is not described here - that is held centrally: cc-devthrottle workflow instructions mission.

Why this exists

The owner watches the session rail on a Director all day, and the Cockpit and phone rosters beside it. He noticed the colours on the rail did not match the Cockpit and asked for a comparison of what all three session cards hold and how each decides its colour.

Two things came out of that review, and the second is the larger one.

The colour disagreement is a version gap with a specific cause. The Gateway folds the colour once and pushes down a colour name; the Director resolves that name to a pixel using a table its own build shipped with. When the fold gained the colour cyan in v2.4.0, every Director older than that lost the ability to render it - the unknown name falls to the magenta broken sentinel. A session the Wingman judged finished therefore paints magenta on an older rail and cyan everywhere else. The sentinel is meant to mean "the push seam is broken", so an old build silently borrows an alarm that belongs to a different fault.

Underneath it, nothing owns what a session card holds. Colour, words and triage bucket are folded once on the Gateway and rendered verbatim, and that has held up - the three screens cannot disagree about what a state means. The field list has no such owner: three files decide it by hand. The review found nine fields living on exactly one of the three screens, and no screen a superset of another. The rail - the surface that is open all day - was the poorest of the three.

What is true when this is finished

  • A Director too old to know a colour says so honestly, in a neutral that cannot be mistaken for a state, and the magenta sentinel means only the one thing it was built to mean.
  • The desktop can answer "what does this colour mean?" without leaving the app, in the Gateway's own words, like the other two surfaces.
  • No screen writes its own sentence about a session's state. Every user-visible word about what a session is doing comes from the Gateway.
  • The three cards carry the same facts, each card is shorter than it is today, and a field added to one surface fails a test on the other two until somebody decides.

The rulings

Settled with the owner across two rounds of a dev report before any code was written. Rulings marked inferred were NOT stated by him - they are the Architect's calls, recorded as such so nobody later reads them as his.

His words An unknown colour paints a neutral. Never a guessed pixel.

"I don't like painting the hex. That's just stupid. If we don't know the color, we could leave the color blank ... just make it very, very light gray so you can still see the dot, with the square, but don't print text."

The Architect's first proposal was to push the Gateway's resolved hex down the seam so an old build could paint the right pixel. The owner rejected it and he was right: a build painting a colour it has never heard of has no legend entry for it, no words, and nothing to hover - it would look correct and mean nothing. The neutral keeps the rule the rest of the product already follows, which is never to guess.

Cost of getting it wrong: the neutral must be visibly a DIFFERENT grey from the palette grey #6B7280, which already means snoozed or exited. A neutral close to it is an affirmative lie that a session is parked - which is precisely why this was magenta in the first place.

His words No surface gives its own reason for anything.

"Nobody should give any local reason for anything. It should always be the gateway ... if you hover over the color, it should show you what that color means."

Wider than the item it was asked about. The rail currently hovers a sentence the Director writes itself, before the Wingman has judged the stop - which is how a calm row can hover the words "needs you". Under this ruling the hover reads the Gateway's stamped label plus the legend, and the local sentence stops being rendered at all.

His words The Wingman's narration line comes off the Cockpit.

"We don't want the rail line on there, that's too much on the card. I never use it - also on the cockpit. I think we should remove that on the cockpit."

Reverses the review's own recommendation, which had been to add it to the other two surfaces. It is the longest thing a card can carry. It is removed from the roster card only - the sentence is still the Wingman's and still read in full where the verdict is.

His words The supervision facts narrow to one cumulative number.

Four numbers - started, open, idle, turns - live on the Cockpit and phone cards. The owner did not know what they were and did not want them on the rail. Asked to make the case, the Architect's honest answer was that three of the four add little, because colour already answers "is this waiting now" and the card already shows how long the current wait has been. The one fact covered nowhere else is cumulative idle: a session that has quietly taken six hours of the owner's waiting across a day looks identical to one that stopped a minute ago. He chose to keep that one and drop the rest. None of the four goes on the rail.

His words Drop the role badge.

"Drop the role glyph, because it doesn't really matter anymore. We don't really use the roles for anything ... it's ownership and parent-child that matters."

Scope warning for whoever builds it: the fold still uses roles internally - whether a stopped session may ask for the owner turns on having a live supervisor, and an Architect is deliberately excluded from that rule. This ruling removes a BADGE from a card. It does not touch the fold.

His words One ranked state slot, on the rail and the Cockpit.

Several state badges can render at once - "not delivered", "winding down", "snooze ended" on the rail, and a row of up to nine chips on the Cockpit. One shows, the most serious, and the hover carries the rest. Asked as two separate items and accepted as both; they are one piece of work applied to two surfaces.

His words Names wrap. Truncating is not acceptable.

"I think it's a good idea to let it wrap as well. Truncating is not a good idea."

His words The cards carry the same identity facts.

The repository on the Cockpit card, which never says which one a session is in. The agent tool and the model as one pill on all three, rather than each surface showing one half. The uncommitted-changes badge on the phone. The machine chip dropped from a phone card that already sits under a machine heading.

His words The Fleet Manager is pinned on the rail.

Accepted with the qualifier that it only matters when a Fleet Manager is in use - which is already how the field behaves. The Gateway decides which row is pinned and writes every word on it; the rail renders that and rules nothing. This is the only field in the mission that must be added to the display-state push.

His words The prompt queue should be visible beyond the Director.

The owner identified this himself. The review had framed it as a missing badge; he pointed out the queue lives on the Director and is not on the Gateway. Checked: the Cockpit already reaches one session's queue correctly, through the Gateway to the owning Director, on its own panel - but the session row the rosters are built from carries no queue count at all, so no card outside the rail can know a session is holding anything. It is one number on the feed, not a synchronisation.

His words There should be a test over the card field list.

"We could do a test. That makes sense to have a test in there."

Without it the mission fixes the drift once and nothing stops it recurring. With it, a field added to one surface fails on the other two until somebody decides.

Inferred The agreement test lands after the cards agree, not before.

He said yes to the test without choosing when. The Architect's call: locking the list before the cards change would mean writing down a list that is known to be wrong and then amending it four times.

Inferred Removals land before additions.

Every change that takes something OFF a card runs as one pass before anything goes on, so the additions land on cards that already have room. Within that pass, letting names wrap comes last - done first it would only make a crowded card taller. The owner ruled on the items, not the order.

Inferred Two wordings the Architect is deciding, not asking.

The neutral value. Settled against docs/VisualStyle.md in both themes, not by the Architect's guess. The binding constraint is the ruling above - obviously distinct from #6B7280.

The word on the surviving number. Standing alone, "idle" reads as "idle right now", which is what the colour already says; the fact being kept is that it is cumulative. It reads "waited", with the existing hover unchanged. Flagged to the owner in the dev report and not objected to, but recorded here as the Architect's call.

The work, in the order it lands

Five phases. Each is a coherent slice and lands as its own pull request. Numbers in brackets are the items from the dev report, kept so the owner's answers stay traceable.

Phase A - the rail tells the truth about colour

Items 1, 3, 6. Touches the desktop only. Needs nothing from the push seam, so it blocks on nothing.

  • An unrecognised colour name paints the neutral instead of the broken sentinel. The sentinel keeps its real job: the Gateway stamped nothing at all while the tunnel is up and settled.
  • A colour legend on the desktop, reading the Gateway's existing legend route and rendering it verbatim. No new wording anywhere.
  • The colour hover reads the legend title plus the Gateway's stamped label. The Director's own locally written reason stops being rendered.

Proved by: a case fed a colour name no build knows, asserting the neutral, and separately that a missing stamp still gives magenta - the two failures must stay distinguishable. For the hover, a session the two writers disagree about (a calm row whose local reason still says it needs the owner), asserting the Gateway's answer wins. A test that only checked the hover was non-empty would pass on the bug.

Also: sweep the rail for any other place it writes its own sentence about a session, and report what is found rather than changing it silently.

Phase B - everything that comes off the cards

Items 9, 7, 13, 2, 5, 15, then 8. One removal pass across all three surfaces.

  • The role badge off the rail.
  • One ranked state slot on the rail and on the Cockpit, the rest in the hover. "Not delivered" reports a loss and already leads on every card, so it outranks the others - the ranking is read from existing behaviour, not invented.
  • The supervision numbers narrow to cumulative idle alone, on the Cockpit and phone. Both read one shared formatter, so this is one edit. The runtime thresholds go with the dropped numbers.
  • The narration line off the Cockpit card.
  • The machine chip off a phone card inside a machine group; it stays in the view where sections mix machines, which is the rule the Cockpit already follows.
  • Last: names wrap instead of truncating, spending the width the rest of the phase frees.

Proved by: a rendering test per surface with every state condition true at once, asserting one badge and that the hover names the others - neither card has such a case today, which is why nobody noticed they could stack. For the wrap, the rail's existing render tests lay a row out at the rail's real opening width; the longest name we can produce must not clip.

Phase C - every card says what the session is

Items 10, 11, 14, 4. The additions, landing on cards Phase B has made room on.

  • The agent tool and the model as one pill on the Cockpit and phone. Both halves come from Gateway folds that already choose their own words, including which of the two absences applies, so no client invents a placeholder.
  • The repository on the Cockpit card.
  • The uncommitted-changes badge on the phone.
  • The pin carried on the display-state push, and the rail reading it.

Proved by: the pin is the one item here that crosses the wire. It must be proved that a Director which does NOT receive the field behaves exactly as it does today - the whole mission exists because a new value reached an old build and became an alarm.

Phase D - the agreement test

Item 11 of the owner's answers. Lands after C, when the three field lists agree.

  • Name the fields a session card carries, and fail when a surface drops one without a stated reason - in the shape of the checks already stopping the three colour tables from drifting.

Proved by: removing a field from one surface and watching the test go red with that field named. A test that has never been watched failing is decoration.

Phase E - the prompt queue

Item 12. The only phase that changes the roster feed rather than a card.

  • A queue count on the session row, stamped like every other card field, then the pill on the Cockpit and phone cards.

Proved by: the same old-build question as the pin - a client that does not receive the count shows what it shows today. And, because this lands after Phase D, the agreement test has to be updated to name the new field, which is the mechanism demonstrating itself.

Out of scope

  • The colour fold itself. No arm, colour name or label changes. The mission changes how an unrecognised name is RENDERED and nothing about how colours are DECIDED.
  • Roles in the fold. A badge is removed from a card. Supervision, the live-supervisor rule and the Architect exception are untouched.
  • Pushing the colour hex down the seam. Explicitly rejected by the owner. Do not reintroduce it as a convenience.
  • The narration line anywhere but the Cockpit card. It is removed there and added nowhere.
  • Started, open and turns. Dropped, not relocated.
  • Other surfaces. The Fleet Map cards, the missions board, the session detail screen and the Voice screen are not in this mission. Only the three roster and rail cards.
  • Upgrading any machine. An older Director shows magenta today; upgrading it is the owner's to do and is not mission work.
  • Colour-blind readability of the ten-colour palette. Raised in the review as worth its own look. Not this mission.

What the review did not cover

Recorded so the next reader does not mistake the review's reach for the product's. The comparison behind this mission read the three cards, the fold, the palette, the legend and the push seam at origin/main. It did not observe a running older Director - the version finding is read off the shipped code at that tag, not off a screen. No performance question was examined.
